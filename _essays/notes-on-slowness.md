There is a kind of looking that takes time to begin. You stand in front of the work and for the first several minutes you are still arriving — still carrying the room you left, the staircase, the coat check. The painting waits.

It is not patience, exactly, that a painting demands. It is something closer to the suspension of purpose.

![Installation view, north-lit gallery interior with diffuse afternoon light.](/assets/images/essays/slowness-fig1.jpg)

The works that hold me longest are always the ones that refuse to explain themselves immediately. They offer something — a surface quality, a color relationship, an edge — and then withhold the rest. You have to earn the rest by staying.

This is not a mystical claim. It is a practical observation about the structure of attention.

![Detail of the surface, showing the worked edge and a thin under-drawing.](/assets/images/essays/slowness-fig2.jpg)

Speed is a form of violence against certain kinds of objects. The museum visitor who photographs every work in a room has not, in any meaningful sense, seen them. The camera documents; the eye understands.

What changes when you slow down is not the object but the quality of the relation between you and the object. The painting does not become more painting. You become more present.

On the wall, the picture holds its silence a little longer than you expect. And then, as with all slow looking, you simply stop — and the room resumes around the image, unchanged and entirely different.
